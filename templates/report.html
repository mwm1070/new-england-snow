01 · The claim

What my uncle actually said

My uncle was born in 1939 and died in 2024. He lived in New England for most of his life, and he complained more than once that there was less snow than there used to be.

Taken seriously, that is a claim about two fixed windows of time. The last six decades of the twentieth century run from the winter of 1940/41 to the winter of 1999/2000. The first two decades of the twenty-first run from 2000/01 to 2019/20. He is saying the second window had less snow than the first.

This report tests that, station by station, on the only public dataset that actually measures snowfall through the whole period: the daily subset of the Global Historical Climatology Network, GHCN-Daily, maintained by NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information.

No. The snow did not decline.

Combining the {{N_STATIONS_REGIONAL}} New England stations with a solid 1941–2000 baseline into a single regional index, seasonal snowfall averaged {{EARLY_LEVEL}} inches per winter in 1941–2000 and {{LATE_LEVEL}} inches in 2001–2020. That is a change of {{DIFF_SIGNED}} inches, in the opposite direction to the complaint.

More importantly, the uncertainty is bigger than the effect. A 95% confidence interval for that change runs from {{CI_LOW}} to {{CI_HIGH}} inches. It includes zero comfortably, and it also includes any decline smaller than about four inches. The data do not support "less snow"; they support "no measurable change".

03 · The core problem

Twenty winters is a small sample of a very noisy quantity

Seasonal snowfall in New England has a standard deviation of about {{EARLY_SD}} inches. That is the key number in this entire report, and it is why the argument cannot be settled by eye.

When a quantity varies that much from winter to winter, an average over twenty winters still carries a standard error of roughly {{LATE_SE}} inches. A real difference smaller than about twice that — say, four inches — is invisible at this sample size. Two decades is not a climate; it is a handful of lucky and unlucky winters.

The honest version

If New England snowfall had fallen by ten inches, this dataset would almost certainly have detected it. If it fell by two or three inches, this dataset could not have detected it — and neither could my uncle's memory. The claim, as stated, is not measurable with the evidence available.

This is not a technicality. It is the whole finding. The complaint and the data are both reasonable, and they are talking past each other because the effect being claimed is smaller than the noise floor of the measurement.

04 · Station by station

Most stations gained snow, not lost it

A regional average can hide as much as it reveals, so the same comparison was run separately at each station that had at least twelve usable seasons in both periods: {{N_PAIRED}} stations in all. Each station was compared only against itself.

{{N_UP}} stations were snowier in 2001–2020 than in 1941–2000; {{N_DOWN}} were less snowy. The median station gained {{MEDIAN_ABS}} inches. The losses are real but scattered: they cluster at a few inland and high-elevation sites, while the gains are spread across the coastal plain and the western valleys.

One caution about reading individual bars: the biggest single change in the set is over thirty inches. Changes that large at a single station usually mean the station moved, the observer changed, or the measurement method changed — not that the local climate shifted that far. This is why the report leans on the regional index and the median rather than on any one station.

05 · Decade by decade

Decades move more than the periods do

If the climate were steadily losing snow, the decade averages should drift downward. They do not. They swing: the 1960s and 1970s were well above the long-run baseline, the 1980s and 1990s were below it, and the 2000s came back to roughly average.

The 1980s sit about eleven inches below the baseline and the 1970s about nine inches above it. Either of those decade-to-decade moves is four times larger than the entire difference between the two study periods. Any narrative built on two particular decades — including a nostalgic one — is mostly picking out the weather of a childhood.

06 · Robustness

The answer does not depend on my choices

Every analysis of this kind involves judgement calls: which months count as winter, and how complete a station's year has to be before it is used. Both were varied deliberately to see whether the conclusion survives.

Three season definitions (July–June, October–May, November–March) were crossed with five coverage thresholds from 50% to 95%, giving {{N_SENS}} separate analyses. All {{N_SENS}} produced a positive point estimate for the change. None produced a trend significant at the 5% level; the smallest p-value was {{SENS_MIN_P}}.

07 · What people remember

Big storms and snow on the ground

Total seasonal snowfall is not the same as what a person notices. Two other measures matter: how often heavy snow fell, and how many days had snow lying on the ground.

Both went up. Days with at least twelve inches of snow in a single day rose from {{DAYS12_E}} to {{DAYS12_L}} per season. Days with at least six inches rose from {{DAYS6_E}} to {{DAYS6_L}}. Days with any snow cover rose from {{COVER_E}} to {{COVER_L}}, a change of {{COVER_DIFF}} days per winter, with a confidence interval of {{COVER_CI}}.

These counts are small and sensitive to how snow depth is measured, so they should not be over-read. But they point the same way as everything else — and they suggest that a memory of fewer snowy days is not supported either.

08 · Why

Warmer and wetter, but not less snowy

Snowfall is a function of two things: how much precipitation falls, and how cold it is when it arrives. Both have moved in New England, in opposite directions for snow.

Seasonal precipitation rose from {{PRECIP_E}} to {{PRECIP_L}} inches, an increase of {{PRECIP_DIFF}} inches, with a confidence interval that excludes zero. Seasonal mean temperature rose from {{TEMP_E}} to {{TEMP_L}} °C.

The extra warmth did not convert the extra moisture into rain everywhere. It more or less cancelled out: more precipitation arrived, a slightly larger share of it fell as rain or mixed precipitation, and the total snowfall stayed level. That is a coherent physical story, and it is consistent with the flat trend rather than contradicting it.

It also explains why a warming climate and a flat snowfall record are not in conflict. Winter in New England still gets cold enough to snow; what changes first is the margin.

09 · A pattern that looks real

The snowiest places did not really lose the most

The stations with the largest losses are also the ones at the highest elevations, which invites a tempting story: perhaps the mountains are losing snow first. The correlation is there — change against elevation gives r = {{CORR_ELEV}}.

It is an artefact. A station that started with a high average can only fall, and the late-period mean is a noisy estimate. Once the starting average is held constant, the elevation correlation collapses to r = {{PARTIAL}}. What looked like a physical pattern is regression to the mean: the usual behaviour of any noisy quantity compared against its own starting point.

The lesson is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to fool yourself here: with twenty-season windows, ranking stations by "who lost the most" mostly ranks them by how lucky their early years were.

10 · What would change the answer

Limits, and the case for my uncle

Three things could overturn or soften this conclusion, and all three are worth stating rather than hiding.

1. The data are not homogenised

GHCN-Daily snowfall is raw station data. It is not adjusted for station moves, observer changes, or changes in how snow is measured. A single station's thirty-inch swing is more likely an institutional change than a climate one. This adds uncertainty in both directions, which means the confidence intervals above are, if anything, too narrow.

2. A different period could give a different answer

These are two specific windows. A comparison of 1961–1990 against 1991–2020, or a trend starting in 1970, would produce different numbers. That is precisely why the pre-registered rule and the robustness sweep matter: they make it harder to pick the window that tells the preferred story.

3. "Less snow" is not the same as "less snow where I was standing"

This analysis covers New England as a region. A person's memory is a sample of one place and a handful of memorable winters, and memory weights the extreme years. It is entirely possible that my uncle's town had a run of poor winters while the region as a whole did not. The data cannot settle that, and neither can this report.

So was he right?

Not in the sense he meant, and not in a way the data can demonstrate. The snow did not decline. But a decline of two or three inches would also have been invisible here — so the honest answer is that his claim is unsupported rather than disproved. He was probably remembering real winters, and generalising from too small a sample, which is what all of us do.

11 · Methods

How the numbers were produced

Data. GHCN-Daily station files from NOAA NCEI, downloaded {{SNAPSHOT}}. {{N_STATIONS_DL}} stations in Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts, Vermont, New Hampshire and Maine had snowfall records spanning both study periods. The SNOW, SNWD, PRCP, TMAX and TMIN elements were parsed from the fixed-width .dly format. Values of −9999 were treated as missing, as were values carrying a non-blank GHCN quality flag.

Seasons. A snow year runs from 1 July to 30 June and is labelled by the year containing its January, so season 2001 means the winter of 2000/01. This keeps a single storm inside a single season and still catches October and late-April snow.

Usable seasons. A station-season counts only if at least {{COVERAGE_PCT}} of its days report snowfall. Missing days are never treated as zero; a season missing a fifth of its days would otherwise look like a dry winter.

Two comparisons. The paired comparison keeps stations with at least twelve usable seasons in each period and compares each station against itself. The regional index converts every station-season into an anomaly against that station's own 1941–2000 mean, then averages across whichever stations reported that season (minimum five). The second is the headline, because it uses far more of the record. The two use slightly different station sets — {{N_SHARED}} of the {{N_PAIRED}} stations appear in both — which is why both are reported.

Uncertainty. Confidence intervals come from a moving-block bootstrap with three-season blocks and 5,000 resamples, which respects the mild year-to-year correlation in the series (lag-1 autocorrelation {{RHO}}). The paired comparison resamples stations; the regional comparison resamples seasons. They answer different questions, and the report quotes the second.

Trend. Sen's slope for the magnitude, Mann–Kendall for significance. Both are rank-based methods that tolerate the outliers a century of weather records inevitably contains.

Decision rule. Fixed before analysis, and reported with its four legs so that the reasoning can be checked rather than trusted.

12 · Reproduce it

Running the analysis

Every number and figure on this page is generated by four scripts. No data files are stored in the repository; the first step downloads them, and everything lands in a directory that git ignores.

python3 src/fetch_data.py     # download 67 New England station files
python3 src/parse_ghcn.py     # parse .dly files into per-season aggregates
python3 src/analyze.py        # statistics and decision rule -> build/results.json
python3 src/figures.py        # six figures -> build/figures/
python3 src/build_site.py     # this page -> build/index.html

The build directory is published to GitHub Pages by a workflow, so neither the raw data nor the generated figures are ever committed. Re-running the pipeline against a newer GHCN snapshot may change the last digit or two of the figures above; the conclusion is far less sensitive than that.
